Terms & Privacy

The legal agreement that exists so nobody can pretend they were not warned. Here is what actually happens to your data, written with enough honesty that you might finish reading it.

1. What even is this (and why does it exist)

OrbitOS is a solo hobby experiment built entirely on a smartphone keyboard by one developer who wanted to see how far a single browser tab could be pushed before choking the browser runtime. It is not Microsoft Windows. It is not Linux. It is not macOS. It is not a venture-backed tech startup, and there is no 24/7 enterprise support desk waiting to take your call. There is just one guy tweaking code whenever spare time permits.

Because this is a web-based browser sandbox, expect bugs, performance hitches, unpredictable edge cases, and the occasional total system crash. When something breaks, there is no IT department to rescue you, just a custom BSOD screen and an overhauled Recovery Menu. If you decide to run your business operations or store critical life documents inside a browser tab operating system coded on a phone, that is a personal choice you will have to make peace with.

2. The only telemetry and tracking that exists

Let us be completely clear: we do not care what you type in your terminal, what notes you write, or what pretend commands you execute. We do not have the time, energy, or server infrastructure to spy on your simulated terminal desktop.

The only tracking present on this site is standard Google Analytics. It tells us high-level metrics like how many people opened the page today, what general country they connected from, and whether mobile browser users are having a buggier experience than desktop users. That is it. It cannot see your terminal commands, your API keys, your local files, your installed packages, or your notes. We could not inspect your browser storage even if we felt like looking, which we do not.

3. OrbitDB and local browser storage (where your stuff actually lives)

There is no secret backend server quietly hoarding your documents. OrbitOS 4.4 runs entirely on top of browser IndexedDB through our custom OrbitDB storage engine, expanding local system storage capacity from the ancient 5MB limit up to a generous 512MB quota. Everything stays strictly inside your own web browser. Here is the full inventory of what gets stored in your browser database and why it needs to be there:

A. Identity & Authentication

Completing the initial setup wizard saves your chosen username and hostname so the terminal prompt knows what name to display. If you set a lock screen password, OrbitOS does not save your plain text password because storing unhashed passwords in 2026 is an absolute embarrassment. Instead, the OS passes your password through a SHA-256 cryptographic hash and stores the scrambled digest. If you forget your password, there is no "Forgot Password" link because there is no server to mail you. You will have to reset through the Recovery Menu or clear your browser database.

B. Personal Files & Media (Notes, Tasks, Calendar, Gallery)

Your notes, task list items, and calendar entries are saved as structured data inside IndexedDB. When you upload photos into the photo Gallery app, OrbitOS converts the images into Base64 encoded strings and writes them directly into OrbitDB. This allows your photos to persist across reboots without needing a cloud storage subscription.

C. APT Packages & App Store State

Applications installed via apt install (such as notes, todo, calc, wiki, pulse, gallery, weather, or retro games like Snake, Blackjack, and Tic-Tac-Toe) store their installation state and package metadata in OrbitDB. This ensures installed apps stay accessible in your system menu instead of disappearing every time you refresh.

D. Aesthetics & UI Themes

Every customization choice you make gets saved locally. Whether you choose Design V2 floating windows or classic terminal CLI mode, pick from 15+ built-in themes (Glassmorphism, Fluent UI, Amoled, Glowmorphism, Cyberpunk, Classic), select custom typography fonts, or upload custom desktop wallpapers, your visual preferences are retained so you do not have to redesign your setup every morning.

E. AI Chat Memory & Custom Personas

AI models have terrible memory retention by default. To maintain a coherent multi-turn conversation, OrbitOS stores recent chat history in IndexedDB under orbitos_ai_memory and attaches it to subsequent prompts. Custom AI personas you author are stored in the same local database.

F. Browser History & Media State

The internal Orbit Browser maintains local history logs and bookmarks inside IndexedDB. The Audius Music Player stores your favorite tracks and recent playlists so your audio queue survives system restarts.

G. API Keys & Local Secrets

When you enter a Google Gemini API key into OrbitOS, it stays exclusively in your local browser storage. It is never transmitted to us or stored on any server we control. It travels directly from your browser to Google endpoints when generating responses.

H. Temporary Session Data

Temporary state tracking uses browser sessionStorage. This handles Safe Mode status and tracks consecutive boot failures. If OrbitOS crashes three times in a row during startup, sessionStorage triggers the automatic failover into Recovery Mode. Closing the browser tab wipes this temporary session counter instantly.

4. Encrypted Backups (.orbitbak) and security warnings

OrbitOS 4.4 features a built-in Encrypted Backup & Restore system that exports your entire operating system state into a portable .orbitbak archive file.

When you trigger a backup via the terminal (backup command), Settings, or Recovery Mode, OrbitOS uses the native Web Crypto API (crypto.subtle) to apply AES-GCM 256-bit encryption with PBKDF2 key derivation (100,000 hashing rounds). It bundles 100% of your system state, including notes, tasks, gallery images, custom themes, installed APT packages, AI chat history, API keys, and diagnostic logs, into a single encrypted payload on your local drive.

Security Warning: An unencrypted or weak-passphrased backup archive contains your complete digital footprint inside OrbitOS. If you send your .orbitbak file to someone on Discord or leave it on a public computer, anyone with the passphrase can restore your system and read everything you saved. If you leak your own backup file, that is entirely a user responsibility, not an OrbitOS flaw.

Restoring a backup is a destructive action: it purges active IndexedDB state, decrypts the archive, validates payload integrity, writes the restored keys, and reboots the OS. Tampered or corrupted backup files will be rejected automatically by the cryptographic validator.

5. External APIs (who your browser actually talks to)

OrbitOS is completely client-side, meaning your web browser connects directly to external third-party services. There is no middleman backend server processing your network requests. Here is the exact list of external services your browser communicates with:

  • Google Gemini AI (generativelanguage.googleapis.com): Typing prompts to the AI assistant sends your text input, local chat history, and personal API key directly to Google servers. If system command execution is enabled, basic system state flags are included so the AI knows what terminal operations are valid.
  • Weather & Geocoding: Checking the weather queries GeoJS (geojs.io) to estimate your coarse location based on your IP address, then forwards coordinates to Open-Meteo (api.open-meteo.com) to pull temperature, forecast, and air quality metrics.
  • Wikipedia App: Searching in the Wiki app sends your query directly to Wikipedia endpoints (en.wikipedia.org) to retrieve article summaries, HTML bodies, and thumbnail images.
  • Music Player (Audius API): Searching or playing music connects directly to api.audius.co to search catalog tracks and stream audio straight to your browser.
  • Micro-Services & Utilities: Text translations route through MyMemory (api.mymemory.translated.net), currency conversions query ExchangeRate-API (open.er-api.com), IP lookup commands query IPify (api.ipify.org), QR code generation calls qrserver.com, and RSS feed fetching uses the AllOrigins CORS proxy (api.allorigins.win) to bypass browser cross-origin restrictions.

6. Data volatility, browser wipes, and storage risks

Because every byte of OrbitOS data lives locally in your browser's IndexedDB database, your data is inherently tied to your browser's lifecycle and storage policies.

If you clear your browser browsing data or site storage, OrbitOS will be completely wiped. Furthermore, mobile operating systems and privacy software may silently clean up IndexedDB storage without asking permission when your device runs low on disk space. Additionally, major architectural overhauls to OrbitOS may occasionally require resetting database schemas to ensure system stability.

The Golden Rule: Never store irreplaceable data inside OrbitOS. Do not store sensitive bank logins, cryptocurrency seed phrases, unbacked-up academic papers, or critical personal documents. If your browser wipes local storage and you do not have an encrypted .orbitbak file saved elsewhere, your data is gone forever.

7. Interface modes (Design V2 vs Classic CLI)

OrbitOS 4.4 introduced Design V2, bringing floating window interfaces, smooth animations, and graphical app popups for terminal commands. However, we know terminal purists exist.

If floating graphical windows offend your CLI sensibilities, you can toggle Design V2 off in Settings or switch back to classic text terminal mode and Fastfetch output at any time. Both design systems coexist, and disabling graphical interfaces will never break core terminal functionality.

9. Acceptable use policy (just use it normally)

OrbitOS is hosted on custom domains (booting at orbitos.lol and documented at about.orbitos.lol). It is a free, solo hobby project, so please do not act like a nuisance.

Do not attempt denial of service attacks against the hosting domain, try to inject malicious scripts into the public repository, or attempt to break the infrastructure for others. Since 99% of OrbitOS executes client-side inside your own browser thread, attempting to exploit or overload the OS mostly results in crashing your own browser tab. A truly impressive self-sabotage tactic.
